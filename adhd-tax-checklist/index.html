The ADHD Tax Checklist: 5 Money Leaks to Look for This Week

By the Kind Reset Team

"That subscription service is billing me again because I forgot to cancel it." That line comes from writer Elizabeth Broadbent, in an ADDitude essay about "the costs of life with ADHD" (ADDitude).

People call these costs the "ADHD tax." Understood.org describes it as "the extra effort, resources, and time people with ADHD put into tasks that may be easier for others," adding: "It's not an actual tax people have to pay, but it can still cost money" (Understood). The same article notes it can also be "an emotional cost," like feelings of shame (Understood).

This checklist covers the money side. It isn't a budget, and it won't fix everything. It's five places to look, one at a time, with a small step for each.

🧾 The checklist at a glance

You don't need to do all five. Pick the one that made you wince.

1️⃣ Late fees

What it looks like: a bill that was paid, just not on time. Broadbent lists where these fees come from: "the library," "your utility company, your phone provider, your landlord, your kids' school, your own school" (ADDitude).

Places to look:

  • Your last two bank or card statements, for any line with "late" or "fee" in it
  • Library account, school portals, parking or toll accounts
  • Anything you pay by mail or by hand each month

Small steps:

  • Put every due date in one place. A paper list on the fridge counts.
  • Consider automatic payments, and watch the balance. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says automatic payments "can help you avoid late fees on your bills," but warns that if your balance is too low when a payment is due, "you might have to pay overdraft or nonsufficient funds fees," and that "both the bank and the company might charge you a fee" (CFPB).
  • Ask whether a due date can move. For credit cards, Chase says you "may be able to request to change the payment due date," and that some people shift due dates "to better align with paychecks." It also notes changes can take "a billing cycle or two" (Chase).

2️⃣ Subscriptions and free trials

What it looks like: a charge for something you stopped using months ago, or a "free" trial that quietly became a paid plan. Broadbent writes: "you forgot to turn off auto-renew, and you're on the hook for another year of service you didn't need" (ADDitude).

Trial terms differ. Two examples, from each company's own pricing page as read on November 15, 2026:

TrialCard needed?What happens at the end
Akiflow, 7 daysYes. Its FAQ says "a card is required""The selected subscription begins after the trial unless it is cancelled" (Akiflow pricing)
Sunsama, 14 daysNo. "No credit card required"It "doesn't ask for your credit card until your trial is over" (Sunsama pricing)

So with Akiflow, per its own FAQ, the paid plan starts unless you cancel. With Sunsama, per its own page, the card question comes only after the trial.

Places to look:

  • One month of card and bank statements. Highlight anything that repeats.
  • Your phone's app store subscriptions list
  • Apps or tools you paid for, used for a week, and stopped opening
  • Your email inbox, searching "receipt", "renewal" or "trial"

Small steps:

  • Cancel one thing you don't use. Just one.
  • Before any new trial, find the end date and put a reminder for the day before.
  • After you cancel, check the next statement to make sure the charge stopped. The CFPB also suggests you "monitor your account to make sure the amount and timing of the transfers are what you agreed to" for automatic payments (CFPB).

3️⃣ Returns you never sent back

What it looks like: a bag by the door with something that didn't fit, still unopened weeks later. Broadbent: "We buy clothes that don't fit, then forget to return them" (ADDitude).

Places to look:

  • By the front door, in the car, in the closet
  • Your order emails from the last 30 days

Small steps:

  • Find the return window for one item today. Write the last day on the bag.
  • Print the label or set up the drop-off now, while the bag is in your hand.
  • Watch for "final sale." Laura Key, writing for Understood (she hosts its ADHD Aha! podcast), describes buying her kids new winter coats she didn't need, on final sale so she "couldn't return them." She put that one at "about $150" (Understood).

4️⃣ Buying it twice

What it looks like: replacing something you lost, or buying something you already own because you forgot you had it. Broadbent: "You bought a pack of pens, forgot you bought a pack of pens, and bought another pack of pens" (ADDitude). Laura Key's coat story above is the same leak. Key writes: "Then I realized I'd already bought coats for them the previous year, a size up so they'd still fit" (Understood).

Places to look:

  • The junk drawer, the bathroom cabinet, the pantry: count the duplicates
  • Things you replace often (chargers, keys, glasses cases)

Small steps:

  • Check before you buy. In the same Understood article, Laura Key suggests a shopping list. Key writes: "the act of making the list forces me to take a mini-inventory of what I have" (Understood).
  • Leave it in the cart overnight. Also from Key: "Leaving items in my cart overnight helps me pump the brakes" (Understood).
  • Give often-lost things one home. A hook for keys, one bowl for chargers.

5️⃣ Last-minute costs

What it looks like: paying extra because something waited until the last moment. Broadbent's examples: "You put off that trip to the post office, so you pay extra for priority shipping," and "you forgot to buy tickets. Now you're paying scalper prices" (ADDitude).

Places to look:

  • Shipping upgrades on recent orders
  • Gifts and events in the next month: birthdays, weddings, school things

Small steps:

  • Name the next deadline now. Write down one gift, form or ticket you'll need in the next 30 days.
  • Do the smallest first step today: pick the gift, fill in the first line of the form, check the ticket date.

⏱️ How to use this list without it turning into a project

A checklist like this can become another system you set up once and abandon. So keep it small:

  1. Pick one leak. Start with the one you noticed first.
  2. Set a 10-minute timer. Look in the places listed for that leak, and do one small step.
  3. Stop when the timer rings. Even mid-task. The other four leaks will still be here next week.

If you fall off, use the line from our reset sheet: "I'm not starting over. I'm picking up where I am: one timer, three things."

🩺 When it's more than a checklist

This list is a set of places to look, not financial or medical advice. If money stress feels constant, if late payments are piling up, or if shame around money is heavy, it's worth talking to someone qualified: a nonprofit credit counselor, an accountant, or your doctor or a therapist for the emotional side.
