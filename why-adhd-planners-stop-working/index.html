Why Every ADHD Planner Works for a Week (and What to Do Instead)

By the Kind Reset Team

You bought the planner. You downloaded the app. You spent a Sunday afternoon color-coding it. It worked for five days, then you missed one, and now the app feels like a disappointed parent you avoid making eye contact with.

That last image isn't ours. It comes from a thread on r/ADHD where someone wrote that their app store history is "a graveyard of productivity apps I was CONVINCED would be 'the one,'" and that "one morning I wake up and the app feels like my disappointed mother so I just… stop opening it" (r/ADHD). The post drew 102 comments.

If that's you, the planner probably wasn't the problem. The pattern is.

🔁 The loop nobody designs for

Many planning systems quietly assume you'll use them every day. That assumption is the weak spot. Here's the loop people describe:

  1. Set up. A burst of energy goes into building the perfect system. One person on X put it bluntly: "I have abandoned Notion, Todoist, Obsidian, Asana, ClickUp, and Trello. Every single one died because I spent 3 hours building a system and then never opened it again" (@adhdfreelancer).
  2. Miss a day. Life happens. A bad night, a deadline, a sick kid.
  3. Shame. Opening the planner now means looking at a list of things you didn't do. Overdue badges. A broken streak.
  4. Quit. Avoiding the planner is easier than facing it. A week later you're shopping for a new one.

None of those steps is about the planner's features. A better app doesn't fix step 3, and a more detailed system can make it worse, because it has more ways to show you what you missed.

So the useful question isn't "which planner works for ADHD?" It's "what happens on the day after I miss a day?"

🧱 Why "more structure" can backfire

When a system falls apart, the instinct is to add more: more categories, more reminders, a habit tracker on top. Here's how that tends to go on a hard day.

What the system asks forWhat it can feel like on a hard day
A long setup before it's usefulYou've spent your energy before doing a single real task
Daily use, tracked with streaksOne missed day "breaks" it, so the whole thing feels ruined
Overdue tasks that pile up in redOpening the app means facing a wall of failure
Doing it aloneNobody notices if you stop, so stopping is silent and easy

None of this is a character flaw. It's a design mismatch, and you can fix a lot of it by changing what the system expects of you.

🛠️ What to do instead: a system built for the day after

Here's a lighter setup. It works on paper, in the notes app you already have, or inside a planner you already own. The point is that it has a built-in way back in.

1. Cap setup at 10 minutes

If your system takes longer than 10 minutes to set up, cut it back. Pick one place your tasks live. That's the setup.

2. Plan three things, not thirty

Each morning (or whenever your "morning" is), write down three things. Make the first one small enough to start right now. If you finish all three, you can pick more.

Three is few enough to be realistic on a rough day, and it makes you choose instead of copying the whole to-do list forward.

3. Add a "Rolled over, no guilt" box

Anything you didn't get to goes into a box labeled Rolled over, no guilt. Not "overdue". Not red. Our reset sheet puts it this way: "Didn't get to it? Write it here and it moves to tomorrow. That's the plan working, not failing."

4. Replace streaks with "Done counts"

Streaks punish you for one bad day. Instead, keep a row of tick boxes. The sheet's rule is: "Tick a box after each reset. No dates, no streak." Because there are no dates, there's nothing to fall behind on.

5. Keep one line for after a missed day

Have one sentence ready for the day after you fall off. This is the one on our sheet:

"I'm not starting over. I'm picking up where I am: one timer, three things."

Put it at the top of your planner or pin it in your notes app. When you come back after a gap, read it first, before you look at anything else. Then do exactly what it says: one timer, then three things.

6. Ask "Who can sit with me?"

On r/ADHD_Programmers, one person put the real need plainly: "We don't need a better planning app, we need someone to look at us in the eye in real life..." (r/ADHD_Programmers).

Some people find tasks easier to start when someone else is around. This is often called body doubling. You don't need anyone to help with the task, just to be there. Free options come first:

  • A friend on a video or phone call, both doing your own thing
  • Sitting at the kitchen table while someone else in the house does their thing
  • Working in a library or a café

⏱️ The "one timer": a 10-minute reset

When the house is part of what fell apart, here's the timer the restart line refers to. It's page 1 of our sheet:

  1. Set a timer for 10 minutes. Grab one bag for trash. That's all the setup there is.
  2. Trash first. Walk the room with the bag. Anything that is clearly trash goes in. Skip anything you'd have to think about.
  3. Dishes second. Carry every cup, plate and bowl to the sink. Washing them is a job for another timer.
  4. One surface third. Pick the first table, counter or nightstand you see. Clear it. Things with a home go home. Everything else goes in one pile for later.
  5. When the timer rings, stop. Even mid-task. Stopping on time is part of the reset, and it can make the next one easier to start.
  6. Done counts: Tick a box after each reset. No dates, no streak. A gap is just a gap.

🌅 What the morning after looks like

Say you missed four days. Here's the whole restart, start to finish:

  1. Open your planner. Read the line first: "I'm not starting over. I'm picking up where I am: one timer, three things."
  2. Move everything left over into Rolled over, no guilt. Don't sort it. Don't reread it.
  3. One timer: do one 10-minute reset.
  4. Three things: write three for today. Make the first one small enough to start right now, and start it.
  5. Tick a box in Done counts. Stop there if you need to.

🩺 When it's more than a planner problem

A lighter system helps with the "miss a day, quit" pattern. It isn't treatment. If the shame or overwhelm feels heavy most days, or you're not sure whether ADHD is part of what's going on, talking with a doctor, therapist or other licensed clinician is a good next step.
